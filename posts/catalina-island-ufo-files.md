If you've ever taken the boat over to Avalon, you probably weren't thinking about flying saucers. But a handful of people in the 1940s and '50s were, and the government kept the paperwork.

![The Avalon waterfront and pier lit up at night](posts/img/catalina/header.jpg)
*The Avalon waterfront at night. Photo: Don Ramey Logan / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0*

On Oct. 23, the Department of War put out its seventh batch of declassified records on unidentified anomalous phenomena, or UAP, the government's current term for UFOs. There are 28 files in the release, and four of them are about Catalina. None include photos, radar data or anything physical. They're old typewritten forms and a handwritten letter, and the government still lists all four as unresolved.

The releases are part of a program called PURSUE, short for the Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters. It got rolling after President Donald Trump announced in February that he'd have the Pentagon and other agencies start digging out their UFO files. The first batch dropped May 8, and new ones have been coming every few weeks at war.gov/UFO. Everything in the archive is a case the government says it couldn't pin down, and the Pentagon's stated goal is to let people look at the originals and "make up their own minds."

So here's what's in the Catalina files.

## The four Catalina reports

**April 28, 1949, Avalon.** The oldest one is a handwritten letter from an Avalon man to Kenneth Arnold, the pilot whose 1947 sighting in Washington is where the phrase "flying saucer" came from. The man wrote that around 8 p.m., his wife and a schoolteacher watched six objects cross the sky, "not in a group, but singly." A few minutes later, over at his daughter's place on the next street, the family spotted four more heading the same way. Somehow the letter ended up in an Air Force file, and the record card attached to it just says "insufficient data for evaluation."

![Project 10073 record card for the April 28, 1949 Catalina sighting](posts/img/catalina/doc-1949-avalon.jpg)
*The Air Force record card for the 1949 Avalon report. Source: U.S. Department of War, PURSUE Release 07 (DOW-UAP-D181)*

**June 25, 1952, Camp Fox.** This is the one most people are talking about. Three Boy Scouts at summer camp were hiking up one of the island's mountains around lunchtime when they saw a silver object at an unknown altitude, moving "faster than a conventional jet." They didn't hear a thing. They said it made what looked like a climbing turn and then disappeared "by disintegration." The Air Force didn't write it up until two months later, filing the report out of March Air Force Base on Aug. 28, which is why you'll see some outlets date this one to August. The report points out the boys had no instruments and that nobody tried to judge how reliable they were. According to the California Post, the scouts drew a sketch on the back of the original report, and nobody has been able to find it.

![Record card and Air Intelligence Information Report for the 1952 Camp Fox sighting](posts/img/catalina/doc-1952-boy-scouts.jpg)
*The record card and Air Intelligence Information Report for the Boy Scouts' 1952 sighting. Source: U.S. Department of War, PURSUE Release 07 (DOW-UAP-D179)*

**Nov. 16, 1953, seen from the mainland.** A man who worked around jets at Long Beach Air Force Base looked out toward the island and saw a round, purplish-white object about the size of a pea. It sat still over Catalina for a bit, then drifted northwest toward Los Angeles in a straight line for about 10 minutes before fading out. The Air Force thought he was a pretty reliable witness. Astronomers at Griffith Observatory pointed out that planets show up more clearly after a rain, and the object's movement didn't match the winds that night. The record card's verdict: Jupiter.

![Project 10073 record card for the Nov. 16, 1953 Catalina sighting, concluding Jupiter](posts/img/catalina/doc-1953-jupiter.jpg)
*The 1953 record card, with its "Astronomical (Jupiter)" conclusion. Source: U.S. Department of War, PURSUE Release 07 (DOW-UAP-D178)*

**March 15, 1955, Avalon.** The last one is short. An Avalon man saw a very bright, round object about the size of a nickel held at arm's length. It went from orange to red and dropped straight down, and the whole thing lasted about two seconds. The Air Force called it a probable meteor, which sounds about right.

![Project 10073 record card for the March 15, 1955 Avalon sighting, listing a probable meteor](posts/img/catalina/doc-1955-meteor.jpg)
*The 1955 record card: "Probable meteor." Source: U.S. Department of War, PURSUE Release 07 (DOW-UAP-D177)*

## Catalina's UFO history goes back further

These files aren't the island's first brush with this stuff. The Catalina Islander reported that on July 8, 1947, just two weeks after Arnold's famous sighting, three Army Air Forces veterans visiting the island saw six objects over Avalon. One of them figured they were going around 850 mph. That story isn't part of the government release, but it shows Catalina has been on the UFO map for a long time.

## What else is in the release

The Catalina files are the old-school part of this batch. The newer material is getting most of the attention, especially a June 2026 report from federal agents near an unnamed national security site out West. They said they watched a flat black triangle, somewhere around 9 to 15 feet across, rise over a ridge, hover, and then take off without a sound. They saw no engines and no exhaust. Both an AARO memo and a signed statement the agents gave the FBI cover it. The batch also includes a 2010 report on a Utah ranch most people know as Skinwalker Ranch.
