There's been plenty of talk about the developing El Niño, and a lot of people are already bracing for a soaker of a winter in Southern California. I'm not convinced. I think several competing signals in the atmosphere and the ocean could get in the way of the typical El Niño storm track, especially for Southern California.

You'll see this year compared to famous El Niño winters like 1982, 1997 and 2015. As far as I can see, none of those years had an atmosphere set up like this one. So here's what I'm watching, and where I think this winter could go.

## The big players: the polar vortex, SSW and the -PDO

Three things could complicate the El Niño signal this winter:

- **The polar vortex (PV)**, the band of cold air circling the Arctic high in the atmosphere.
- **Possible sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) events**, which can knock the vortex around and shift where the jet stream and blocking patterns set up, and so where storms end up.
- **A continuing negative Pacific Decadal Oscillation (-PDO)**, a long-running pattern in the North Pacific that changes the background the storms form in.

Rather than a steady parade of storms aimed at Southern California, I'm watching for a pattern that sends storms into Northern California and the Pacific Northwest more often. The result could be a winter with big differences in rainfall from one end of California to the other.

## NorCal and the Pacific Northwest

I'm leaning toward Northern California and the Pacific Northwest getting the more favorable share of the storms this winter.

That does **not** mean Southern California gets shut out. I fully expect some significant storms to make their way south. I just think the storm track could favor NorCal and the Northwest more often than a textbook El Niño would suggest.

## Southern California: my rainfall call

For downtown Los Angeles, I'm looking at **22 to 25 inches** of rain for the season. That's a respectable year, but I don't expect it to stay wet.

I'm looking for several significant storms, some with impressive rainfall totals, separated by much quieter stretches. Expect dry, warm spells and Santa Ana wind events too. Don't be surprised if we go from watching a major storm to dealing with offshore winds, sunshine and fire-weather concerns.

That's Southern California weather: feast and famine.

My rainfall calls for the season:

- **Downtown Los Angeles:** 22–25 inches
- **San Francisco:** 32–35 inches
- **San Diego:** 13–16 inches

## Temperatures: don't expect a consistently cold winter

Rain and temperature are two different forecasts. I'm not expecting a consistently cold winter across the West. I expect stretches of warmer-than-average weather, especially between storms, with occasional cold intrusions when the jet stream shifts and lets colder air drop south.

For Southern California, that could mean above-average warmth followed by sharp cool-downs when storms come through. Add Santa Ana events and it could be a real temperature roller coaster.

## Big Bear: a much better second half of winter

For Big Bear, Snow Summit, Bear Mountain, Snow Valley and the rest of the San Bernardino Mountains, I'm expecting an inconsistent start, then a much better chance at colder storms after New Year's.

January, February and possibly March should bring several colder storm cycles into Southern California. They won't necessarily be back to back. I'm expecting a pattern that swings between warmer, drier periods and cold intrusions that give the local resorts chances at natural snow. When one of those cold systems lines up with enough Pacific moisture, we could see some good snowfall.

Don't write off Big Bear or the local ski season just because the early part of winter runs warm.

## The Sierra Nevada: going out on a limb

This is where I'm taking a bigger gamble:

- **Above 8,000 feet:** 500+ inches of snow for the season
- **Below 8,000 feet:** 350–400 inches

I think the Sierra could have an impressive winter, especially up high, even if the storms come unevenly. There's plenty of room for some major individual storm cycles. It all comes down to how much sustained cold air reaches the West Coast and the Sierra.

## The Northeast: warmer stretches, but keep the snow shovels out

Back East, I'm leaning toward periods of above-average temperatures through the winter, likely influenced by swings in the polar vortex.

But I'm not writing off winter storms. I'm watching for several chances at coastal Nor'easters, with the southern jet stream connecting with the polar jet along the Eastern Seaboard. The right mix of blocking, cold air and coastal storm development could produce significant snow from the Mid-Atlantic into New England.

Timing and cold air will be critical. A Nor'easter can be a big snow producer, but without enough cold air in place, snow can quickly change to rain, especially along the coast.

## My overall winter call

- 🌧️ **California:** more frequent storms in Northern California and the Pacific Northwest.
- 🌧️ **Rainfall:** downtown LA 22–25 inches, San Francisco 32–35, San Diego 13–16.
- ❄️ **Sierra Nevada:** 500+ inches above 8,000 feet; 350–400 inches below.
- 🏔️ **SoCal mountains:** warmer, drier stretches early, then several colder storm chances after New Year's for Big Bear and the local resorts.
- 🌡️ **SoCal temperatures:** above-average warmth broken up by colder storms and Santa Ana events.
- ❄️ **Northeast:** milder stretches interrupted by a few potentially significant Nor'easters.

I'm not buying the idea that El Niño automatically means a blockbuster Southern California winter. The ocean may be signaling El Niño, but the atmosphere gets the final vote.

These are preliminary numbers, and there's plenty of time for the pattern to change. I'll be watching the stratosphere, Pacific ocean temperatures, model ensemble trends and the jet stream through November and December.

One thing I will say: don't confuse an El Niño winter with a guaranteed wet winter for Southern California. They are not the same thing.
